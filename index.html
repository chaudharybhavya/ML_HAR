Can a phone on your waist tell what you are doing, even if it has never seen you before?

We classify 2.56-second windows of accelerometer and gyroscope readings into six everyday activities, and judge every model on people it was not trained on. Bhavya Chaudhary (25110075) and Lakshika Aswal (25110179).

In short: a regularised linear model (lasso logistic regression) recognises the six activities of new people about 95% of the time, against 0.821 macro-F1 for a baseline that uses only the direction of gravity and the amount of movement. It never confuses moving with being still and never misses lying down. Its remaining mistakes are fine distinctions (sitting vs standing, walking vs stairs), and they are concentrated in a few people whose postures or gait differ from the people it learned from. On a different phone, worn by one of us, it scores 0.860: the same two confusions grow, and for sitting vs standing we can trace the reason to how the phone was tilted. Testing on windows from people the model has already seen would have hidden most of these mistakes, and that comes mainly from sharing people, not from the overlap between windows.

How this page reasons, from the question to the conclusions

  1. The question is about new people, so the person, not the window, is the unit we must generalise over.
  2. The data has overlapping windows and people who move differently, so a random split would leak. The 561 features are highly redundant.
  3. Preprocessing is deliberately small: standardisation only, learned from the training people of each fold.
  4. Evaluation uses 7 person-wise folds for every choice, keeps 9 test people for one final test, and scores with macro-F1.
  5. Models: a majority-class baseline and a 4-number physics baseline, then logistic regression, an RBF SVM and a random forest, all tuned on the same folds.
  6. The final model is chosen by a rule fixed in advance (the simplest model within one standard error of the best), before the test set is opened.
  7. One final test gives the score and an interval over people; the error analysis shows where, for whom and why it fails.
  8. A blind test on our own phone, a table of what changes the result, the limitations, and the conclusions.

2. The data and the physics behind it

Bhavya

UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset (Anguita et al., 2013; licence CC BY 4.0). 30 volunteers wore a Samsung Galaxy S II on the waist. Recordings are cut into overlapping windows: 7,352 training windows from 21 people and 2,947 test windows from 9 people. Each window comes with 561 engineered features and with the raw signals (3-axis accelerometer in g, 3-axis gyroscope in rad/s). The data is public; we do not redistribute it (see Reproduce).

The volunteers were 19–48 years old and followed a scripted protocol in a lab; the authors labelled the activities by hand from video. The authors also made the split into training and test people (a random 70% / 30% of the volunteers), and we kept it.

What the accelerometer measures: gravity plus motion

An accelerometer cannot tell gravity apart from movement; it measures their sum. The dataset authors separate them with a 0.3 Hz low-pass filter: the slow part is gravity (which way the phone points, so posture), and the fast part is body motion (movement).

3. The 561 features and PCA

Lakshika

The 561 features are a few ideas (how much the body moves, the rhythm and frequency of the movement, the orientation) repeated over 3 axes, 2 sensors, and time vs frequency domain. Only 53 describe gravity. They overlap heavily: 8,206 pairs have |r| > 0.9 (207 above 0.99), and 431 features have a near-copy.

Decision: logistic regression on 100 components scores 0.920 vs 0.933 on all 561 features, so we do not put PCA in front of the models. PCA keeps the largest variation, which is not the same as the most useful variation.

Details and code in the repository: 02_features_pca.ipynb.

4. Preprocessing: what was done to the data, and why

Both

Some preprocessing was already applied by the dataset's authors, and we could only check it. The rest are our own choices. The rule behind all of them: anything that is learned from data (scaling, PCA, feature selection, model weights) is fitted on the training people of the fold only. Only looking (plots, counts, checks) used all 21 training people, and nothing used the 9 test people.

Already done by the dataset's authors

  1. Noise filtermedian filter, then a 20 Hz low-pass filter
  2. Gravity split0.3 Hz low-pass = gravity; body = total − gravity
  3. Windows2.56 s (128 readings), 50% overlap
  4. Features561 statistics per window, time and frequency domain
  5. Scalingeach feature to [−1, 1]

We verified two of these steps on the raw signals. The 50% overlap: 95.5% of consecutive windows share half their readings (section 2). The gravity split: our own 0.3 Hz filter reproduces the dataset's gravity signal to within 0.002 g (section 10).

5. Evaluation design: test on people, not on windows

Bhavya

A random split of windows puts near-copies (overlapping neighbours) and the same people on both sides, so it measures “recognising people already seen”. This is a known trap in sensor and clinical studies: record-wise cross-validation can report much better accuracy than subject-wise cross-validation, and only the subject-wise version matches use on new people (Saeb et al., 2017). Our question is about new people, so we split by person: GroupKFold with 7 folds of 3 people each. Each model trains on 18 people and is scored on the other 3, seven times. The folds are saved to a file and every model uses the same ones, which makes comparisons fair. The 9 test people were kept out of all tuning and choosing.

The metric: macro-F1

For one activity, precision asks: of the windows the model called “sitting”, how many really were? Recall asks: of the windows that really were sitting, how many did the model find? F1 combines the two, and macro-F1 averages F1 over the six activities:

F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)     macro-F1 = (F1walking + … + F1lying) / 6

Every activity counts equally, so a model cannot look good by ignoring a hard one. Accuracy can: a model that always says “lying” gets accuracy 0.19 but macro-F1 only 0.05. That is our first baseline; section 6 adds a physics baseline. We report accuracy next to macro-F1 for the final results.

How we handle uncertainty

  • When choosing: the 7 fold scores give a mean and a standard error (standard deviation ÷ √7). Settings or models closer than one standard error are treated as tied, and the simpler one wins (the one-standard-error rule, section 6).
  • For the final score: a bootstrap that resamples the 9 test people, because a person's windows are not independent (section 8).

Which leak matters more? Removing the overlapping neighbours from training lowers the random-split score only from … to …, still far above … for new people. So most of the inflation comes from seeing the same people, not from the 50% overlap. Splitting by person is what matters; dropping neighbours alone would not make a random split honest. One caveat: removing neighbours also removes about a quarter of the training windows, so the small drop is, if anything, an upper bound on what the overlap is worth.

6. The baselines and the models we compared

Three candidate models, from simple to flexible, each tuned with the same person-wise folds. Within each model we chose a setting with the one-standard-error rule: find the best mean score, and among all settings within one standard error of it (SE = spread over the 7 folds ÷ √7), pick the simplest. Differences smaller than that are noise from which people landed in which fold.

More complex was not better here. The linear model ties with the SVM and beats the forest. The engineered features already make the activities close to linearly separable, and the remaining errors come from differences between people, which a more flexible model does not remove.

Bhavya

Logistic regression

Gives each activity a score that is a weighted sum of the 561 features, and turns the six scores into probabilities with softmax. It is fitted by maximum likelihood plus a penalty on large weights: ridge (shrinks all weights) or lasso (sets many to exactly zero). The assumption is linear boundaries between activities. We reproduced one prediction by hand: for a standing window of an unseen person the scores were 16.4 for standing and 6.6 for sitting (the physically similar posture), giving 99.99% standing.

Lakshika

RBF support vector machine

Drops the linear assumption: the boundary can bend, with flexibility set by gamma (how local each training window's influence is) and C (how much mistakes are penalised). Gamma matters far more than C: at gamma = 0.1 every window looks unlike every other and the model collapses to 0.06. The model keeps 2,133 of 6,294 training windows as support vectors (one fold), most of them sitting (538) and standing (493): the hardest pair needs the most boundary examples. It ties with lasso (0.931 vs 0.934) and varies less between people (fold std 0.027 vs 0.036).

Lakshika

Random forest

Many decision trees, each on a bootstrap sample with random feature subsets, voting together. Needs no scaling. The settings barely matter (all 27 between 0.891 and 0.909). Fewer features per split is slightly better (23 features: 0.905–0.909; 168 features: 0.891–0.894): the trees then differ more from each other, so the vote averages out more of their mistakes. We did not use the out-of-bag score, because overlapping windows from the same people would leak into it.

What tuning taught us

  • Ridge: every C from 0.01 to 100 is within noise of the best. Differences between people (fold scores 0.885–0.981) are far larger than differences between settings.
  • Lasso matches the best score using about a third of the features (161–190 of 561 at C = 0.1). But which features it keeps changes from fold to fold, because many features are near-copies, so we make no “most important features” claims.
  • Random forest importance agrees: impurity and permutation importance share only 1 feature in their top 15, and shuffling any single feature costs at most 0.0014 macro-F1. Shuffling one gravity feature together with its 4 near-copies costs 0.196. Importance belongs to groups of correlated features, not to single features.

Details and code in the repository: 04_logistic.ipynb, 05_svm.ipynb, 06_random_forest.ipynb; every fold score of every setting is in results/cv_*.csv.

7. Choosing the final model

Bhavya

The three candidates (each model's one-SE choice) were compared fold by fold on the same people. The same one-SE rule across models gives a threshold of 0.934 − 0.014 = 0.920: lasso (0.934) and SVM (0.931) qualify, the forest (0.907) does not. Lasso vs SVM cannot be told apart (SVM is better on 2 of 7 folds, by at most 0.022), so we took the simpler one: lasso logistic regression, C = 0.1 (linear, about 180 features). The decision was written down before the test set was opened.

Why resample people? A person's windows are strongly correlated (neighbours overlap, and every window carries that person's posture and gait), so the real sample size is 9 people, not 2,947 windows. With only 9 people, the honest claim is a range of roughly 0.91–0.97. For context only (run after the decision): the SVM scored 0.947 and the forest 0.919. The SVM–lasso gap of 0.002 is far inside that uncertainty.

For context, the dataset's authors report 96% test accuracy on the same 2,947 test windows with a multiclass SVM (Anguita et al., 2013). Our 94.6% is in the same range, from a linear model whose every choice was made on the training people.

10. A blind test on our own phone

Lakshika

Everything above comes from one dataset, recorded in one lab with one phone. To see whether the model holds up outside it, Bhavya recorded all six activities on a different phone, and we ran the final model on those recordings. The model and its setting were fixed (section 7) before the recordings were made, and nothing was tuned on them.

From a phone recording to UCI-style features

  1. Loadphyphox CSV; m/s² → g, gyroscope in rad/s
  2. Cleaneven time grid, median filter, 20 Hz Butterworth
  3. Resampleto 50 Hz, like UCI
  4. Split gravity0.3 Hz Butterworth (causal); body = total − gravity
  5. Window2.56 s, 50% overlap; first and last 5 s cut
  6. Features451 of the 561, on UCI's [−1, 1] scale

We checked the pipeline on UCI's own raw training signals before using it on a phone:

  • 451 of the 561 features are reproduced (correlation ≥ 0.99 with the dataset's values). The other 110 cannot be: the dataset does not define its entropy, its gravity-magnitude columns are copies of the body-magnitude ones, and some gravity and high-frequency jerk features are unstable.
  • Our 0.3 Hz filter gives the dataset's gravity signal to within 0.002 g (median), using a causal filter (a zero-delay filter is about 4× further off).
  • Logistic regression on our recomputed 451 features scores 0.931, vs 0.933 on all 561 provided features.

The phone loses accuracy in the same two blocks where the test people made their mistakes: the walking types (here upstairs, mostly called “walking”) and sitting vs standing. Walking, downstairs and lying stay near-perfect, and moving vs still is confused only twice in 270 windows.

Why upstairs is the weakest activity

Of the 43 upstairs windows, 13 were called walking, 4 downstairs and 1 lying. Two things explain much of this.

  • Mixed labels. The staircase had a short landing (about two flat steps) after roughly every ten stairs. The whole recording is labelled “upstairs”, but some of its windows contain flat walking, so for those windows “walking” is partly right.
  • Forward lean. Landings happened throughout the recording, but the errors did not: 18 of the first 29 windows are wrong, and the last 14 (from 42 s on) are all correct. In the correctly recognised windows the phone leaned further forward (mean gravity z 0.43 g) than in the misrecognised ones (0.36 g; flat walking: 0.27 g). So the model seems to tell climbing from walking largely by this lean, and when the climber stays more upright it sees walking.

Why sitting vs standing fails: the tilt of the phone

UCI training: sittingUCI training: standing● phone sitting · ■ phone standing (red = wrong)

For the 21 training people, sitting and standing differ mostly along gravity y (averages: sitting +0.14 g, standing −0.16 g). On this phone, worn on the side of the hip, both postures sit at y ≈ 0, right on the border between the two clouds. They differ only along z (0.34 vs 0.17 g), a direction in which the training people's postures overlap. So the tilt the model relies on is nearly the same for both postures. This resembles what section 9 found for test people 9 and 10.

Is the model's confidence still trustworthy?

When the model is very confident it is still almost always right on the new phone. It is just very confident less often, and it is unsure in the right places: its mean confidence is lowest for upstairs and sitting.

Context only: smoothing over consecutive windows

An app sees a stream of windows, so it could report the majority vote of the last few windows instead of each window alone. On the phone recordings this raises macro-F1 from 0.860 to 0.870 (last 3 windows) and 0.886 (last 5). This is not part of the final model and was not used to choose anything.

Caveat. This is one person, one phone, one session, with one recording per activity. There is nothing to resample that would say how the model does for other people or phones, so we give no interval. The result shows that the model transfers, and how it fails; it does not estimate how well it works for people in general.

12. Limitations

  1. Who it learned from: 21 adults doing scripted activities in a lab with one phone model at one position. Other placements, phones, ages and free-living behaviour are untested here, apart from our one-person phone test (section 10).
  2. Unfamiliar postures fail confidently. The model only knows postures like its training people's.
  3. Few people. 9 test people give a wide interval (0.906–0.975), and individuals range from 0.83 to 1.00.
  4. Correlated errors. Windows within a bout and a person are not independent evidence.
  5. One window at a time. Activities last many windows; smoothing predictions over time could fix isolated errors. It is not part of the final model and was only tried as context on our phone recordings (a vote over the last 5 windows: 0.860 → 0.886).
  6. No transitions (e.g. standing up) in the six classes.
  7. Prediction, not explanation. Feature choices and importances are unstable among correlated features, so we make no causal claims.

13. Conclusions

Both

The question: can a phone on the waist tell which of six activities a person it has never seen is doing?

Our answer: largely yes, under the dataset's conditions. A lasso logistic regression reaches macro-F1 0.945 on 9 unseen people (95% interval over people 0.906–0.975), against 0.821 for a 4-number physics baseline and 0.05 for always guessing the most common activity.

What the evidence supports

  1. How you test decides what you learn. The same model scores 0.985 with a random split and 0.933 when whole people are held out. Removing overlapping windows barely changes this (0.977): the inflation comes mainly from having seen the same people. Only the person split answers our question, and the cross-validation estimate built on it (0.934) is consistent with the final test (0.945).
  2. A simple, regularised linear model is enough. It ties with the RBF SVM and beats the random forest. With 21 training people the two best models cannot be told apart, so the choice followed a rule fixed in advance, not the test score.
  3. The average hides people. Test people range from 0.83 to 1.00, and three of the nine make 71% of the errors.
  4. The errors are physical, not random. The model never confuses moving with still and never misses lying. It fails where the signals themselves are close: sitting vs standing (a small tilt) and the three ways of walking.
  5. Confidence helps, up to a point. At 90% confidence or more the model is right 99% of the time, but it is also confidently wrong on postures unlike any in its training people.
  6. It transfers to another phone only partly. On our own recordings it scores 0.860, with the same two confusions enlarged, and how the phone sits on the hip explains the sitting vs standing failures.

What we do not claim

  • That 0.945 holds for other phone positions, other populations, or unscripted daily life.
  • That any single feature is “the important one”, or that the model explains why an activity looks the way it does. It predicts.
  • That the phone result (one person, one session) estimates performance for people in general.

What we would do next

Collect more people and more phone placements, since differences between people limit the result far more than the choice of model; add features aimed at the sitting vs standing tilt; and test smoothing over time on recordings that include switches between activities.

Changes from our proposal

  • PCA before the SVM: planned, then dropped, because PCA lowered the score of the model we tested it with (section 3).
  • Lasso to show “which features carry the signal”: its choices change from fold to fold among near-copy features, so we report how many features it needs, not which ones (section 6).
  • Leave-one-person-out over all 30 people: not run. Over all 30 it would use the test people for evaluation design; over the 21 training people, the 7-fold out-of-fold predictions already score every person, and 21 more slow lasso fits would add little.
  • External test: the proposal described recordings on our own phones. We recorded one person (Bhavya) on one phone. Instead of a separate simple-feature model, we reproduced 451 of the 561 features so that the phone model stays close to the final model (section 10).
  • Split explorer: it shows the score, misclassified windows and fold spread for the three splits, but not a separate confusion matrix for each split. The confusion matrix is shown for the final test instead (section 9).
